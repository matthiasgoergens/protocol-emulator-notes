# Prior art: programming, steering and controlling chaotic/randomly-wired discrete networks

Context: our "crazy network" is a fixed, randomly-wired ring of 64 8-bit cells (37 extra sparse links: local, medium-range, or anywhere), each cell running a programmable op (add/XOR/sub/max) and constant, with an output tap, stepping rate, per-line reset, and per-line CPU-injected seed bytes, feeding a composite-video colour palette from the tap cell's top 4 bits. Host-side search is cheap; on-chip programmability is what a "program" or a "player" can touch. This note surveys fields that program, steer, or analyse chaotic/complex discrete dynamics, with a concrete "how we'd use it" for each.

## 1. Reservoir computing

**Echo State Networks (ESN).** Jaeger's 2001 technical report ("The echo state approach to analysing and training recurrent neural networks", GMD Report 148, freely available as a PDF from his faculty page) showed that a large, fixed, randomly-connected recurrent network with the "echo state property" (inputs eventually wash out past state) can be driven by an input signal and read out with a single *linear* trained layer, without ever training the recurrent weights. "Programming" here means: fix the reservoir, choose an input encoding, and fit a cheap readout.

**Liquid State Machines (LSM).** Maass, Natschläger & Markram, "Real-time computing without stable states: a new framework for neural computation based on perturbations", *Neural Computation* 14(11), 2002 (open PDF at the authors' TU Graz page, igi-web.tugraz.at/PDF/189.pdf), generalise this to spiking/continuous-time networks: transient perturbations of a "liquid" carry the input's history, and a separate readout extracts task-relevant state at each moment.

**CA reservoirs.** Yilmaz, "Reservoir Computing using Cellular Automata" (arXiv:1410.0162, 2014) replaces the liquid with an elementary cellular automaton: an input is XORed/OR'd into initial cell states, the CA is iterated a few steps, and the resulting space-time volume is fed to a linear or logistic readout. This is the closest existing analogue to our chip — a fixed, cheap, local-rule substrate driven by external input and read out downstream.

*How we'd use it:* treat our ring exactly as a CA reservoir. Fix a "program" (ops/constants/tap) once, and use the *per-line seed bytes* as the reservoir's time-varying input instead of as arbitrary state resets. Precompute (host-side, offline) a small linear or lookup-table map from a handful of tap-cell values (or several taps if we can read more than one line's worth) to a target trajectory — e.g. "seed sequence right maps to a rising ramp of hues". This reframes the seed bytes as a *trained input encoding* rather than noise, which is exactly the missing "principled handle" we lack today. Caveat: our tap is a lossy scalar view (top 4 bits, one cell), so the readout is nearly free (there is no trainable readout weight on real hardware) — the encoding-into-seeds is where the leverage has to go.

## 2. Controlling chaos with tiny perturbations

**OGY.** Ott, Grebogi & Yorke, "Controlling Chaos", *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 64, 1196 (1990), DOI 10.1103/PhysRevLett.64.1196 (PDF at yorke.umd.edu). Key idea: a chaotic attractor is densely filled with unstable periodic orbits; nudging *one* accessible system parameter, by a small amount, at the right moments (near a Poincaré-section fixed point) can lock the trajectory onto any of these orbits, turning chaos into a chosen periodic behaviour, using only local linearisation (no full model needed).

**Pyragas delayed feedback.** Pyragas, "Continuous control of chaos by self-controlling feedback", *Phys. Lett. A* 170(6), 421–428 (1992), DOI 10.1016/0375-9601(92)90745-8 (author's own PDF at privat.ftmc.lt/pyragas). Feeds back the difference between the current state and a delayed copy of itself (delay = target orbit's period) as a small correction term — no state-space model needed at all, just a delay line and a gain.

**Synchronisation.** Pecora & Carroll, "Synchronization in Chaotic Systems", *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 64, 821 (1990), DOI 10.1103/PhysRevLett.64.821. Shows that coupling one chaotic system's output into a drive term of a second (or driving a subsystem with a common signal) can lock the two onto the same trajectory when the "sub-Lyapunov exponents" are negative — the basis of chaotic communication schemes and of "master–slave" visual effects.

**Targeting.** Related work (Shinbrot, Ott, Grebogi, Yorke, early 1990s) shows how a tiny perturbation, applied while a chaotic trajectory happens to pass near a desired region, can steer it there exponentially faster than waiting for a natural recurrence — a "just nudge it when it's already close" strategy.

*How we'd use it:* our per-line reset and per-cell constant are exactly OGY/Pyragas-style small perturbations applied to an already-running chaotic ring. Rather than resetting the whole ring on a fixed schedule, host-side precompute (from a simulated run) the handful of scanlines where the tap cell's trajectory passes near a target value, and inject a one-byte nudge (a seed byte, or a momentary constant change) only there — a delayed-feedback variant is to feed the tap value from *n* lines ago back in as a per-line constant, which is directly implementable with the existing "seed bytes injected by the CPU" mechanism and costs nothing extra in silicon. This could turn "slow melt into a weave" into "locked-in weave, on demand", i.e. a controllable rather than accidental effect — worth trying because it uses only knobs we already have.

## 3. Evolving and analysing CAs / random Boolean networks

**Langton's λ / edge of chaos.** Langton, "Computation at the edge of chaos: phase transitions and emergent computation", *Physica D* 42(1–3), 12–37 (1990). λ measures the fraction of a CA's rule table that maps to non-quiescent output; interesting (Class IV, particle-carrying) behaviour clusters near a critical λ between frozen (Class I/II) and chaotic (Class III) regimes.

**Kauffman's random Boolean networks (RBN).** Kauffman's classic model (*The Origins of Order*, 1993) of N randomly-wired binary nodes converges to attractor cycles; critical/near-critical connectivity produces a "frozen core" of nodes stuck at fixed values plus a small (~N^(1/3)–N^(2/3)) set of "relevant" nodes that actually vary — the rest of the network is dead weight.

**Mitchell, Crutchfield & Das.** Their evolved 1-D CAs for the density-classification task, analysed via "embedded-particle" computational mechanics (Crutchfield & Hordijk, "Embedded-particle computation in evolved cellular automata"; Mitchell, "Computation in Cellular Automata: A Selected Review", melaniemitchell.me/PapersContent/ca-review.pdf), show that evolved rules compute by propagating "particles" through background "domains" — the same particle/domain language that MAP-Elites-found "colour cycling" and "melt into a weave" effects are probably instances of.

**Wolfram's four classes** (fixed point, periodic, chaotic, "complex"/Class IV) give a coarse taxonomy for triaging random programs before a full evolutionary run.

**Wuensche's Discrete Dynamics Lab (DDLab).** Wuensche, "Basins of attraction in cellular automata", *Complexity* 5(6), 2000; DDLab (1993–2017) visualises whole basin-of-attraction *trees* — which states funnel into which cycles — and a derived "Z parameter" that predicts a rule's qualitative behaviour class from its rule table alone, without running it.

*How we'd use it:* our ring is effectively a randomly-wired network on {add, XOR, sub, max}/GF(2)∪tropical operations rather than pure Boolean logic, but the same machinery applies. Host-side, compute a λ-like statistic per program (fraction of "expansive" vs "contracting" ops, weighted by the sparse graph's mixing time) and use it to *pre-filter* random programs before spending MAP-Elites budget on them — this directly attacks the "80% noise" problem by throwing away the frozen/fully-chaotic bulk before evaluating it on real hardware or even in simulation. Separately, running a DDLab-style basin-of-attraction analysis on the ring's actual (small, since state is 64×8 bits — too big for exhaustive enumeration, but samplable) transition graph could reveal whether "melt into a weave" is a long transient into an attractor, which would explain why it is *slow* and suggest where to seed to reach it faster.

## 4. Differentiable approaches

**Neural Cellular Automata (NCA).** Mordvintsev, Randazzo, Niklasson & Levin, "Growing Neural Cellular Automata", *Distill* (2020), DOI 10.23915/distill.00023, distill.pub/2020/growing-ca — a tiny (~8k-parameter) per-cell update rule, backprop-trained end-to-end through many simulated steps, learns to grow and self-repair a target image from a single seed pixel. Follow-up: Niklasson, Mordvintsev, Randazzo & Levin, "Self-Organising Textures", *Distill* (2021), distill.pub/selforg/2021/textures, trains the same kind of rule against a Gram-matrix texture loss instead of a pixel target, and gets rules that synthesise textures rather than pictures.

**Differentiable logic gate networks.** Petersen et al., "Deep Differentiable Logic Gate Networks", NeurIPS 2022 (arXiv:2210.08277, github.com/Felix-Petersen/difflogic) — fixed random *connectivity* between neurons (exactly our situation: wiring is fixed), each neuron has a *learned, softened distribution over which 2-input logic gate it implements* (AND/XOR/etc.), trained by gradient descent, then hardened to the single most likely gate at inference/synthesis time.

**Lenia.** Chan, "Lenia and Expanded Universe", ALIFE 2020 proceedings (direct.mit.edu/isal/.../isal2020) and "Lenia: Biology of Artificial Life" (2019) — a continuous-state, continuous-time generalisation of Conway's Life with smooth kernels and growth functions, producing hundreds of stable, glider-like "species"; differentiable end-to-end, so gradient search over kernel/growth-function parameters is a live technique for finding stable patterns.

*How we'd use it:* differentiable logic gate networks are the most directly transferable idea, because our wiring is *also* fixed at tapeout and our "program" is *also* per-cell op selection from a small discrete menu (add/XOR/sub/max) — precisely a "learned distribution over gate choice" problem, just with 4-ary arithmetic ops instead of 16 logic gates. Build a differentiable *software* twin of the ring (soft/relaxed versions of add/XOR/sub/max, e.g. via a temperature-weighted mixture, akin to a Gumbel-softmax over the 4 choices per cell) and backprop a chosen visual objective (e.g. a differentiable proxy for "hue changes at rate R when gamepad axis X moves") through it to get per-cell op/constant assignments — then harden and burn onto real per-cell registers. NCA-style training (grow toward a *target time-series* of tap-cell colours, not a static image) is the natural loss shape for a racing-the-beam video chip, and is worth trying before hand-designed objectives because it directly optimises the temporal signal we actually display, sidestepping the "single-objective evolution gamed into stripes" failure by using a much richer, per-scanline target rather than one scalar fitness.

## 5. Hyperdimensional / vector-symbolic computing

Kanerva, "Hyperdimensional Computing: An Introduction to Computing in Distributed Representation with High-Dimensional Random Vectors", *Cognitive Computation* 1(2), 2009 (Springer, open PDF via Semantic Scholar/author mirrors), and Plate, "Holographic Reduced Representations", *IEEE Trans. Neural Networks* 6(3), 1995. Both show that structured computation — binding, bundling, permutation — can be built purely from *random* high-dimensional vectors with XOR (Kanerva) or circular convolution (Plate) as the binding operator, with the randomness itself guaranteeing approximate orthogonality/invertibility (a "blessing of dimensionality" instead of a curse).

*How we'd use it:* our ring is small (64 cells × 8 bits ≈ 512 bits, not "hyper"-dimensional), but the *design principle* transfers: use XOR-binding of a small set of fixed random "codebook" constants (baked into per-cell constants) with the CPU's per-line seed bytes to *address* particular sub-patterns without needing per-pattern custom wiring — e.g. bind a gamepad-derived seed with a stored "palette-shift" vector via XOR at specific cells, then let the ring's own dynamics unbind it downstream (VSA's bundling/unbinding is literally XOR/permutation, both of which our cells already support as an op or as tap selection). This is a plausible route to "a few gamepad parameters steering the effect": treat one or two per-line seed bytes as VSA-bound control vectors rather than raw noise.

## 6. Evolvable hardware and open-ended search

**Thompson's evolved FPGA circuits.** Thompson, "An Evolved Circuit, Intrinsic in Silicon, Entwined with Physics", ICES 1996 (Springer LNCS 1259; PDF via ResearchGate/damninteresting.com summary "On the Origin of Circuits"). A genetic algorithm evolved a 10×10 unclocked FPGA region's configuration bits to discriminate 1 kHz vs 10 kHz tones; the winning circuit exploited undocumented analogue coupling and temperature-dependent parasitics of the *specific physical chip*, working only in the lab's temperature range and not resimulatable in a clean digital model — a sharp warning about overfitting to one instance of "random" hardware.

**Miller's Cartesian Genetic Programming (CGP).** Genotypes are grids of nodes wired to earlier nodes/inputs by index, each choosing a function from a small library — originally built for evolving digital circuits, later generalised to arbitrary programs (Miller, various, incl. "Cartesian Genetic Programming", Springer 2011 book chapter).

**Novelty search.** Lehman & Stanley, "Abandoning Objectives: Evolution through the Search for Novelty Alone", *Evolutionary Computation* 19(2), 2011, DOI 10.1162/evco_a_00025 (open PDF at gwern.net and the authors' own pages). Rewards behavioural novelty alone, sidestepping deceptive fitness landscapes where the objective's gradient leads to dead-end local optima — directly the "single-objective evolution gamed into boring stripes" failure mode.

**MAP-Elites**, Mouret & Clune, "Illuminating search spaces by mapping elites", arXiv:1504.04909 (2015) — already in use here.

**Picbreeder / CPPNs.** Secretan, Beato, D'Ambrosio, Rodriguez, Campbell & Stanley, "Picbreeder: evolving pictures collaboratively online", CHI 2008, and the journal follow-up "Picbreeder: A Case Study in Collaborative Evolutionary Exploration of Design Space" — users interactively bred Compositional Pattern-Producing Networks (CPPN, a NEAT variant), and crucially could *resume evolving other users' published images*, building deep lineages no single session would find.

*How we'd use it:* Thompson's result is a direct caution for us — a program discovered by evolution/MAP-Elites *on a simulated model* of our fixed random wiring may exploit simulator quirks (rounding, overflow wraparound semantics) that don't match the real chip; always validate top MAP-Elites hits against RTL/gate-level sim, not just a fast behavioural model, before calling them findings. Novelty search is worth running *alongside* the existing single-objective evolution (not instead of it) specifically to escape the "boring stripes" attractor — reward being different from everything already collected, then hand-curate as we do now with MAP-Elites. A Picbreeder-style *persistent, resumable* archive (let a session start from anyone's previously-found effect and keep mutating it, rather than each MAP-Elites run starting cold) would probably compound our "few striking effects" faster than independent runs.

## 7. Controllability of nonlinear/discrete systems

**Boolean network control.** Work on "control kernels" (e.g. Gates, Correia, Wang & Rocha and related; see arXiv:2010.12075, "The basis of easy controllability in Boolean networks") shows that a minimal *pinned* subset of nodes — held at fixed values — can steer an entire random Boolean network into a chosen attractor; control-kernel size tends to scale with the log of the number of attractors, i.e. good news for us since it suggests only a few pinned cells might suffice.

**Koopman operator control.** Data-driven Koopman methods lift a nonlinear system's state into a (higher-dimensional) space of observables in which the dynamics become approximately *linear*, so standard linear control (LQR, MPC) can be applied back on the lifted coordinates; see e.g. arXiv:2412.01085 for a recent data-driven optimal-control treatment. Unsure how well this scales to our discretised, non-smooth (mod-256 arithmetic, integer max) dynamics, since Koopman theory is developed for continuous, typically smooth systems — flag this as the shakiest fit in this note.

*How we'd use it:* the control-kernel idea maps almost literally onto our per-line reset mechanism — instead of resetting the *whole* ring, host-side search for a small subset of cells (perhaps 3–6 of 64) whose *forced* value, if held fixed via seed injection across several lines, reliably drives the rest of the ring into a chosen visual attractor (e.g. "colour-cycling" mode vs "weave" mode), giving exactly the "few gamepad parameters" knob we're missing — each gamepad axis pins a different small subset. Koopman/linear-lifting is worth a cheap try (fit linear dynamics to a handful of monitored cell values over many lines, see if prediction error stays low) but should not be trusted without checking a real-hardware validation run; if the fit is poor, drop it rather than forcing a bad model.

## Ranked shortlist: five experiments to try first

1. **Control-kernel search for gamepad knobs.** Simulate the fixed wiring off-host; for each of a handful of found "interesting" MAP-Elites programs, search for small (≤6-cell) subsets whose pinned value (via seed injection) reliably switches the ring between two or more known attractor-behaviours. *Measure:* does pinning reproduce the switch in >90% of random initial states within N lines? *Success* = one seed byte reliably toggles between two named effects — the first real "knob".
2. **Delayed-feedback (Pyragas) seeding.** Feed the tap cell's value from k lines ago back as next line's seed/constant, sweep k and gain. *Measure:* does the tap-cell trajectory lock onto a short cycle (autocorrelation spikes at period k) instead of wandering? *Success* = a tunable "lock strength" knob that turns chaos into a controllable-period visual loop.
3. **λ-style pre-filter before MAP-Elites.** Compute a cheap per-program statistic (fraction of contracting ops: max/AND-like vs XOR/add; estimated graph mixing time) and check correlation against the existing "80% noise" judgement labels. *Measure:* AUC of this statistic as a noise-vs-interesting classifier on already-labelled programs. *Success* = statistic cuts evaluation budget substantially (e.g. rejects most of the 80% noise for <5% false-reject of known-good programs).
4. **Differentiable soft-op relaxation for a temporal target.** Build a JAX/PyTorch soft relaxation of add/XOR/sub/max over the exact fixed graph, optimise per-cell op-mixture + constants against a target tap-colour time series (e.g. "sine sweep of hue"), harden, and simulate on the real integer/RTL model. *Measure:* does the hardened program reproduce the target time series to within visual tolerance, and does gradient search reach usable programs faster than MAP-Elites generations? *Success* = a program found in minutes of GPU time that MAP-Elites needed hours to approximate, or a genuinely new controllable effect.
5. **Persistent, resumable archive (Picbreeder-style).** Store every MAP-Elites elite in a searchable, taggable archive keyed by behaviour descriptor, and always seed new evolutionary runs from the archive rather than from scratch. *Measure:* rate of new distinct effects found per compute-hour before vs after switching to warm starts. *Success* = a measurable increase in the discovery rate of "striking effects worth curating" per unit of host compute.

Everything above is reported prior art, not measured on our hardware — items 6 (Thompson) and 7 (Koopman) carry the most uncertainty in fit; validate any hardware-adjacent claim (control-kernel size, λ correlation) against our own simulator before relying on it.
